0402: Section 6.2 Interchange of limits
0403: Note: 1–2.5 lectures, subsections on derivatives and power series (which requires Section 2.6) optional.
0404: 🔗
0405: Large parts of modern analysis deal mainly with the question of the interchange of two limiting operations. When we have a chain of two limits, we cannot always just swap the limits. For instance,
0406: \begin{equation}
0407: 0 =
0408: \lim_{n\to\infty}
0409: \left(
0410: \lim_{k\to\infty}
0411: \frac{n}{n + k}
0412: \right)
0413: \neq
0414: \lim_{k\to\infty}
0415: \left(
0416: \lim_{n\to\infty}
0417: \frac{n}{n + k}
0418: \right)
0419: = 1 .
0420: \end{equation}
0421: 🔗
0422: When talking about sequences of functions, interchange of limits comes up quite often. We look at several instances: continuity of the limit, the integral of the limit, the derivative of the limit, and the convergence of power series.
0423: 🔗
0424: Subsection 6.2.1 Continuity of the limit
0425: If we have a sequence \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) of continuous functions, is the limit continuous? Suppose \(f\) is the (pointwise) limit of \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\text{.}\) If \(\lim_{k\to\infty} x_k = x\text{,}\) we are interested in the following interchange of limits, where the equality to prove is marked with a question mark. Equality is not always true. In fact, the limits to the left of the question mark might not even exist.
0426: \begin{equation}
0427: \lim_{k \to \infty}
0428: f(x_k)
0429: =
0430: \lim_{k \to \infty}
0431: \Bigl(
0432: \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x_k)
0433: \Bigr)
0434: \overset{?}{=}
0435: \lim_{n \to \infty}
0436: \Bigl(
0437: \lim_{k \to \infty}
0438: f_n(x_k)
0439: \Bigr)
0440: =
0441: \lim_{n \to \infty}
0442: f_n(x)
0443: =
0444: f(x) .
0445: \end{equation}
0446: We wish to find conditions on the sequence \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) so that the equation above holds. If we only require pointwise convergence, then the limit of a sequence of functions need not be continuous, and the equation above need not hold.
0447: 🔗
0448: Example 6.2.1.
0449: Define \(f_n \colon [0,1] \to \R\) as
0450: \begin{equation}
0451: f_n(x) \coloneqq
0452: \begin{cases}
0453: 1-nx & \text{if } x < \nicefrac{1}{n},\\
0454: 0 & \text{if } x \geq \nicefrac{1}{n}.
0455: \end{cases}
0456: \end{equation}
0457: See Figure 6.4.
0458: 🔗
0459: Figure 6.4. Graph of \(f_n(x)\text{.}\)
0460: 🔗
0461: Each function \(f_n\) is continuous. Fix an \(x \in (0,1]\text{.}\) If \(n \geq \nicefrac{1}{x}\text{,}\) then \(x \geq \nicefrac{1}{n}\text{.}\) Therefore, for \(n \geq \nicefrac{1}{x}\text{,}\) we have \(f_n(x) = 0\text{,}\) and so
0462: \begin{equation}
0463: \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = 0.
0464: \end{equation}
0465: On the other hand, if \(x=0\text{,}\) then
0466: \begin{equation}
0467: \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(0) =
0468: \lim_{n \to \infty} 1 = 1.
0469: \end{equation}
0470: Thus the pointwise limit of \(f_n\) is the function \(f \colon [0,1] \to \R\) defined by
0471: \begin{equation}
0472: f(x) \coloneqq
0473: \begin{cases}
0474: 1 & \text{if } x = 0,\\
0475: 0 & \text{if } x > 0.
0476: \end{cases}
0477: \end{equation}
0478: The function \(f\) is not continuous at 0.
0479: 🔗
0480: 🔗
0481: If we, however, require the convergence to be uniform, the limits can be interchanged.
0482: 🔗
0483: Theorem 6.2.2.
0484: Suppose \(S \subset \R\text{.}\) Let \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) be a sequence of continuous functions \(f_n \colon S \to \R\) converging uniformly to \(f \colon S \to \R\text{.}\) Then \(f\) is continuous.
0485: 🔗
0486: 🔗
0487: Proof.
0488: Let \(x \in S\) be fixed. Let \(\{ x_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) be a sequence in \(S\) converging to \(x\text{.}\)
0489: 🔗
0490: Let \(\epsilon > 0\) be given. As \(\{ f_k \}_{k=1}^\infty\) converges uniformly to \(f\text{,}\) we find a \(k \in \N\) such that
0491: \begin{equation}
0492: \babs{f_k(y)-f(y)} < \nicefrac{\epsilon}{3}
0493: \end{equation}
0494: for all \(y \in S\text{.}\) As \(f_k\) is continuous at \(x\text{,}\) we find an \(N \in \N\) such that for all \(m \geq N\text{,}\)
0495: \begin{equation}
0496: \babs{f_k(x_m)-f_k(x)} < \nicefrac{\epsilon}{3} .
0497: \end{equation}
0498: Thus for all \(m \geq N\text{,}\)
0499: \begin{equation}
0500: \begin{split}
0501: \babs{f(x_m)-f(x)}
0502: & =
0503: \babs{f(x_m)-f_k(x_m)+f_k(x_m)-f_k(x)+f_k(x)-f(x)}
0504: \\
0505: & \leq
0506: \babs{f(x_m)-f_k(x_m)}+
0507: \babs{f_k(x_m)-f_k(x)}+
0508: \babs{f_k(x)-f(x)}
0509: \\
0510: & <
0511: \nicefrac{\epsilon}{3} +
0512: \nicefrac{\epsilon}{3} +
0513: \nicefrac{\epsilon}{3} = \epsilon .
0514: \end{split}
0515: \end{equation}
0516: Therefore, \(\bigl\{ f(x_m) \bigr\}_{m=1}^\infty\) converges to \(f(x)\text{,}\) and consequently \(f\) is continuous at \(x\text{.}\) As \(x\) was arbitrary, \(f\) is continuous everywhere.
0517: 🔗
0518: 🔗
0519: 🔗
0520: Subsection 6.2.2 Integral of the limit
0521: Again, if we simply require pointwise convergence, then the integral of a limit of a sequence of functions need not be equal to the limit of the integrals.
0522: 🔗
0523: Example 6.2.3.
0524: Define \(f_n \colon [0,1] \to \R\) as
0525: \begin{equation}
0526: f_n(x) \coloneqq
0527: \begin{cases}
0528: 0 & \text{if } x = 0,\\
0529: n-n^2x & \text{if } 0 < x < \nicefrac{1}{n},\\
0530: 0 & \text{if } x \geq \nicefrac{1}{n}.
0531: \end{cases}
0532: \end{equation}
0533: See Figure 6.5.
0534: 🔗
0535: Figure 6.5. Graph of \(f_n(x)\text{.}\)
0536: 🔗
0537: Each \(f_n\) is Riemann integrable (it is continuous on \((0,1]\) and bounded), and the fundamental theorem of calculus says that
0538: \begin{equation}
0539: \int_0^1 f_n =
0540: \int_0^{\nicefrac{1}{n}} (n-n^2x)\,dx = \nicefrac{1}{2} .
0541: \end{equation}
0542: Let us compute the pointwise limit of \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\text{.}\) Fix an \(x \in (0,1]\text{.}\) For \(n \geq \nicefrac{1}{x}\text{,}\) we have \(x \geq \nicefrac{1}{n}\) and so \(f_n(x) = 0\text{.}\) Hence,
0543: \begin{equation}
0544: \lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = 0.
0545: \end{equation}
0546: We also have \(f_n(0) = 0\) for all \(n\text{.}\) So the pointwise limit of \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) is the zero function. In summary,
0547: \begin{equation}
0548: \nicefrac{1}{2} =
0549: \lim_{n\to\infty}
0550: \int_0^1 f_n (x)\,dx
0551: \neq
0552: \int_0^1
0553: \left(
0554: \lim_{n\to\infty}
0555: f_n(x)\right)\,dx
0556: =
0557: \int_0^1 0\,dx = 0 .
0558: \end{equation}
0559: 🔗
0560: 🔗
0561: But if we require the convergence to be uniform, the limits can be interchanged. 1
0562: Weaker conditions are sufficient for this kind of theorem, but to prove such a generalization requires more sophisticated machinery than we cover here: the Lebesgue integral. In particular, the theorem holds with pointwise convergence as long as \(f\) is integrable and there is an \(M\) such that \(\snorm{f_n}_{[a,b]} \leq M\) for all \(n\text{.}\)
0563: 🔗
0564: Theorem 6.2.4.
0565: Let \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) be a sequence of Riemann integrable functions \(f_n \colon [a,b] \to \R\) converging uniformly to \(f \colon [a,b]
0566: \to \R\text{.}\) Then \(f\) is Riemann integrable, and
0567: \begin{equation}
0568: \int_a^b f = \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_a^b f_n .
0569: \end{equation}
0570: 🔗
0571: 🔗
0572: Proof.
0573: Let \(\epsilon > 0\) be given. As \(f_n\) goes to \(f\) uniformly, we find an \(M \in \N\) such that for all \(n \geq M\text{,}\) we have \(\babs{f_n(x)-f(x)} < \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)}\) for all \(x \in [a,b]\text{.}\) In particular, by reverse triangle inequality, \(\babs{f(x)} < \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)} + \babs{f_n(x)}\) for all \(x\text{.}\) Hence, \(f\) is bounded, as \(f_n\) is bounded. Note that \(f_n\) is integrable and compute
0574: \begin{equation}
0575: \begin{split}
0576: \overline{\int_a^b} f
0577: -
0578: \underline{\int_a^b} f
0579: & =
0580: \overline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) + f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx
0581: -
0582: \underline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) + f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx
0583: \\
0584: & \leq
0585: \overline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx + \overline{\int_a^b} f_n(x) \,dx
0586: -
0587: \underline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx - \underline{\int_a^b} f_n(x) \,dx
0588: \\
0589: & =
0590: \overline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx + \int_a^b f_n(x) \,dx
0591: -
0592: \underline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx - \int_a^b f_n(x) \,dx
0593: \\
0594: & =
0595: \overline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx
0596: -
0597: \underline{\int_a^b} \bigl( f(x) - f_n(x) \bigr)\,dx
0598: \\
0599: & \leq
0600: \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)} (b-a) +
0601: \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)} (b-a) = \epsilon .
0602: \end{split}
0603: \end{equation}
0604: The first inequality is Proposition 5.2.5. The second inequality follows by Proposition 5.1.8 and the fact that for all \(x \in [a,b]\text{,}\) we have \(\frac{-\epsilon}{2(b-a)} < f(x)-f_n(x) < \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)}\text{.}\) As \(\epsilon > 0\) was arbitrary, \(f\) is Riemann integrable.
0605: 🔗
0606: Finally, we compute \(\int_a^b f\text{.}\) We apply Proposition 5.1.10 in the calculation. Again, for all \(n \geq M\) (where \(M\) is the same as above),
0607: \begin{equation}
0608: \begin{split}
0609: \abs{\int_a^b f - \int_a^b f_n} & =
0610: \abs{ \int_a^b \bigl(f(x) - f_n(x)\bigr)\,dx}
0611: \\
0612: & \leq
0613: \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)} (b-a) = \frac{\epsilon}{2} < \epsilon .
0614: \end{split}
0615: \end{equation}
0616: Therefore, \(\bigl\{ \int_a^b f_n \bigr\}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges to \(\int_a^b f\text{.}\)
0617: 🔗
0618: 🔗
0619: Example 6.2.5.
0620: Suppose we wish to compute
0621: \begin{equation}
0622: \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^1 \frac{nx+ \sin(nx^2)}{n} \,dx .
0623: \end{equation}
0624: It is impossible to compute the integrals for any particular \(n\) using calculus as \(\sin(nx^2)\) has no closed-form antiderivative. However, we can compute the limit. We have shown before that \(\frac{nx+ \sin(nx^2)}{n}\) converges uniformly on \([0,1]\) to \(x\text{.}\) By Theorem 6.2.4, the limit exists and
0625: \begin{equation}
0626: \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_0^1 \frac{nx+ \sin(nx^2)}{n} \,dx
0627: =
0628: \int_0^1
0629: x \,dx = \nicefrac{1}{2} .
0630: \end{equation}
0631: 🔗
0632: 🔗
0633: Example 6.2.6.
0634: If convergence is only pointwise, the limit need not even be Riemann integrable. On \([0,1]\) define
0635: \begin{equation}
0636: f_n(x) \coloneqq
0637: \begin{cases}
0638: 1 & \text{if } x = \nicefrac{p}{q} \text{ in lowest terms and } q \leq n, \\
0639: 0 & \text{otherwise.}
0640: \end{cases}
0641: \end{equation}
0642: Each function \(f_n\) differs from the zero function at finitely many points; there are only finitely many fractions in \([0,1]\) with denominator less than or equal to \(n\text{.}\) So \(f_n\) is integrable and \(\int_0^1 f_n = \int_0^1 0 =
0643: 0\text{.}\) It is an easy exercise to show that \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges pointwise to the Dirichlet function
0644: \begin{equation}
0645: f(x) \coloneqq
0646: \begin{cases}
0647: 1 & \text{if } x \in \Q, \\
0648: 0 & \text{otherwise,}
0649: \end{cases}
0650: \end{equation}
0651: which is not Riemann integrable.
0652: 🔗
0653: 🔗
0654: Example 6.2.7.
0655: In fact, if the convergence is only pointwise, the limit of bounded functions is not even necessarily bounded. Define \(f_n \colon [0,1] \to \R\) by
0656: \begin{equation}
0657: f_n(x) \coloneqq
0658: \begin{cases}
0659: 0 & \text{if } x < \nicefrac{1}{n},\\
0660: \nicefrac{1}{x} & \text{else.}
0661: \end{cases}
0662: \end{equation}
0663: For every \(n\) we get that \(\babs{f_n(x)} \leq n\) for all \(x \in [0,1]\) so the functions are bounded. However, \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges pointwise to the unbounded function
0664: \begin{equation}
0665: f(x) \coloneqq
0666: \begin{cases}
0667: 0 & \text{if } x = 0,\\
0668: \nicefrac{1}{x} & \text{else.}
0669: \end{cases}
0670: \end{equation}

0674: Subsection 6.2.3 Derivative of the limit
0675: While uniform convergence is enough to swap limits with integrals, it is not enough to swap limits with derivatives, unless you also have uniform convergence of the derivatives themselves.
0676: 🔗
0677: Example 6.2.8.
0678: Let \(f_n(x) \coloneqq \frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\text{.}\) Then \(f_n\) converges uniformly to 0. See Figure 6.6. The derivative of the limit is 0. However, \(f_n'(x) = \cos(nx)\text{,}\) which does not converge even pointwise. For example, \(f_n'(\pi) = {(-1)}^n\text{.}\) Moreover, \(f_n'(0) = 1\) for all \(n\text{,}\) which does converge, but not to \(0\text{.}\)
0679: 🔗
0680: Figure 6.6. Graphs of \(\frac{\sin(nx)}{n}\) for \(n=1,2,\ldots,10\text{,}\) with higher \(n\) in fainter gray.
0681: 🔗
0682: 🔗
0683: Example 6.2.9.
0684: Let \(f_n(x) \coloneqq \frac{1}{1+nx^2}\text{.}\) If \(x \neq 0\text{,}\) then \(\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(x) = 0\text{,}\) but \(\lim_{n \to \infty} f_n(0) = 1\text{.}\) Hence, \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges pointwise to a function that is not continuous at \(0\text{.}\) We compute
0685: \begin{equation}
0686: f_n'(x) = \frac{-2 n x}{(1+ n x^2)^2} .
0687: \end{equation}
0688: For every \(x\text{,}\) \(\lim_{n\to\infty} f_n'(x) = 0\text{,}\) so the derivatives converge pointwise to 0, but the reader can check that the convergence is not uniform on any interval containing \(0\text{.}\) The limit of \(f_n\) is not differentiable at \(0\)—it is not even continuous at \(0\text{.}\) See Figure 6.7.
0689: 🔗

0692: 🔗
0693: See the exercises for more examples. Using the fundamental theorem of calculus, we find an answer for continuously differentiable functions. The following theorem is true even if we do not assume continuity of the derivatives, but the proof is more difficult.
0694: 🔗
0695: Theorem 6.2.10.
0696: Let \(I\) be a bounded interval and let \(f_n \colon I \to \R\) be continuously differentiable functions. Suppose \(\{ f_n' \}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges uniformly to \(g \colon I \to \R\text{,}\) and suppose \(\bigl\{ f_n(c) \bigr\}_{n=1}^\infty\) is a convergent sequence for some \(c \in I\text{.}\) Then \(\{ f_n \}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges uniformly to a continuously differentiable function \(f \colon I \to \R\text{,}\) and \(f' = g\text{.}\)
0697: 🔗
0698: 🔗
0699: Proof.
0700: Define \(f(c) \coloneqq \lim_{n\to \infty} f_n(c)\text{.}\) As \(f_n'\) are continuous and hence Riemann integrable, via the fundamental theorem of calculus, we find that for \(x \in I\text{,}\)
0701: \begin{equation}
0702: f_n(x) = f_n(c) + \int_c^x f_n' .
0703: \end{equation}
0704: As \(\{ f_n' \}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges uniformly on \(I\text{,}\) it converges uniformly on \([c,x]\) (or \([x,c]\) if \(x < c\)). Thus, the limit as \(n \to \infty\) on the right-hand side exists. Define \(f\) at the remaining points (where \(x\neq c\)) by this limit:
0705: \begin{equation}
0706: f(x) \coloneqq
0707: \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(c) + \lim_{n\to\infty} \int_c^x f_n'
0708: =
0709: f(c) + \int_c^x g .
0710: \end{equation}
0711: The function \(g\) is continuous, being the uniform limit of continuous functions. Hence, \(f\) is differentiable and \(f'(x) = g(x)\) for all \(x \in I\) by the second form of the fundamental theorem.
0712: 🔗
0713: It remains to prove uniform convergence. Suppose \(I\) has a lower bound \(a\) and upper bound \(b\text{.}\) Let \(\epsilon > 0\) be given. Take \(M\) such that for all \(n \geq M\text{,}\) we have \(\babs{f(c)-f_n(c)} < \nicefrac{\epsilon}{2}\) and \(\babs{g(x)-f_n'(x)} < \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)}\) for all \(x \in I\text{.}\) Then
0714: \begin{equation}
0715: \begin{split}
0716: \babs{f(x) - f_n(x)} & =
0717: \abs{\left(f(c) + \int_c^x g\right) - \left( f_n(c) + \int_c^x f_n' \right)}
0718: \\
0719: & \leq
0720: \babs{f(c) - f_n(c)} + \abs{\int_c^x g - \int_c^x f_n'}
0721: \\
0722: & =
0723: \babs{f(c) - f_n(c)} + \abs{\int_c^x \bigl(g(s) - f_n'(s)\bigr) \, ds}
0724: \\
0725: & <
0726: \frac{\epsilon}{2}
0727: +
0728: \frac{\epsilon}{2(b-a)}
0729: (b-a)
0730: =\epsilon. \qedhere
0731: \end{split}
0732: \end{equation}
0733: 🔗
0734: 🔗
0735: The proof goes through without boundedness of \(I\text{,}\) except for the uniform convergence of \(f_n\) to \(f\text{.}\) As an example suppose \(I = \R\) and let \(f_n(x) \coloneqq \nicefrac{x}{n}\text{.}\) Then \(f_n'(x)=\nicefrac{1}{n}\text{,}\) which converges uniformly to \(0\text{.}\) However, \(\{f_n\}_{n=1}^\infty\) converges to 0 only pointwise.
0736: 🔗

0738: Subsection 6.2.4 Convergence of power series
0739: In Section 2.6 we saw that a power series converges absolutely inside its radius of convergence, so it converges pointwise. Let us show that it (and all its derivatives) also converges uniformly. This fact allows us to swap several types of limits. Not only is the limit continuous, but we can also integrate and even differentiate convergent power series term by term.
0740: 🔗
0741: Proposition 6.2.11.
0742: Let \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n {(x-a)}^n\) be a convergent power series with a radius of convergence \(\rho\text{,}\) where \(0 < \rho \leq \infty\text{.}\) Then the series converges uniformly in \([a-r,a+r]\) whenever \(0 < r < \rho\text{.}\)
0743: 🔗
0744: In particular, the series converges (pointwise) to a continuous function on \((a-\rho,a+\rho)\) if \(\rho < \infty\text{,}\) or on \(\R\) if \(\rho = \infty\text{.}\)
0745: 🔗
0746: 🔗
0747: Proof.
0748: Let \(I \coloneqq (a-\rho,a+\rho)\) if \(\rho < \infty\text{,}\) or let \(I \coloneqq \R\) if \(\rho= \infty\text{.}\) Take \(0 < r < \rho\text{.}\) The series converges absolutely for every \(x \in I\text{,}\) in particular if \(x = a+r\text{.}\) So \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty \sabs{c_n} r^n\) converges. Given \(\epsilon >0\text{,}\) find \(M\) such that for all \(k \geq M\text{,}\)
0749: \begin{equation}
0750: \sum_{n=k+1}^\infty \sabs{c_n} {r}^n < \epsilon .
0751: \end{equation}
0752: For all \(x \in [a-r,a+r]\) and all \(m > k\text{,}\)
0753: \begin{multline*}
0754: \abs{\sum_{n=0}^m c_n {(x-a)}^n -
0755: \sum_{n=0}^k c_n {(x-a)}^n}
0756: =
0757: \abs{\sum_{n=k+1}^m c_n {(x-a)}^n}
0758: \\
0759: \leq
0760: \sum_{n=k+1}^m \sabs{c_n} {\sabs{x-a}}^n
0761: \leq
0762: \sum_{n=k+1}^m \sabs{c_n} {r}^n
0763: \leq
0764: \sum_{n=k+1}^\infty \sabs{c_n} {r}^n
0765: <\epsilon.
0766: \end{multline*}
0767: The partial sums are therefore uniformly Cauchy on \([a-r,a+r]\) and hence converge uniformly on that set.
0768: 🔗
0769: Moreover, the partial sums are polynomials, which are continuous, and so their uniform limit on \([a-r,a+r]\) is a continuous function. As \(r < \rho\) was arbitrary, the limit function is continuous on all of \(I\text{.}\)
0770: 🔗
0771: 🔗
0772: As we said, we will show that power series can be differentiated and integrated term by term. The differentiated or integrated series is again a power series, and we will show it has the same radius of convergence. Therefore, any power series defines an infinitely differentiable function.
0773: 🔗
0774: We first prove that we can antidifferentiate, as integration only needs uniform limits.
0775: 🔗
0776: Corollary 6.2.12.
0777: Let \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n {(x-a)}^n\) be a convergent power series with a radius of convergence \(0 < \rho \leq \infty\text{.}\) Let \(I \coloneqq (a-\rho,a+\rho)\) if \(\rho < \infty\) or \(I \coloneqq \R\) if \(\rho= \infty\text{.}\) Let \(f \colon I \to \R\) be the limit. Then
0778: \begin{equation}
0779: \int_a^x f = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{c_{n-1}}{n} {(x-a)}^{n} ,
0780: \end{equation}
0781: where the radius of convergence of this series is at least \(\rho\text{.}\)
0782: 🔗
0783: 🔗
0784: Proof.
0785: Take \(0 < r < \rho\text{.}\) The partial sums \(\sum_{n=0}^k c_n {(x-a)}^n\) converge uniformly on \([a-r,a+r]\text{.}\) For every fixed \(x \in [a-r,a+r]\text{,}\) the convergence is also uniform on \([a,x]\) (or \([x,a]\) if \(x < a\)). Hence,
0786: \begin{equation}
0787: \int_a^x f =
0788: \int_a^x \lim_{k\to\infty} \sum_{n=0}^k c_n {(s-a)}^n \, ds
0789: =
0790: \lim_{k\to\infty}
0791: \int_a^x \sum_{n=0}^k c_n {(s-a)}^n \, ds
0792: =
0793: \lim_{k\to\infty}
0794: \sum_{n=1}^{k+1} \frac{c_{n-1}}{n} {(x-a)}^{n} . \qedhere
0795: \end{equation}
0796: 🔗
0797: 🔗
0798: Corollary 6.2.13.
0799: Let \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty c_n {(x-a)}^n\) be a convergent power series with a radius of convergence \(0 < \rho \leq \infty\text{.}\) Let \(I \coloneqq (a-\rho,a+\rho)\) if \(\rho < \infty\) or \(I \coloneqq \R\) if \(\rho= \infty\text{.}\) Let \(f \colon I \to \R\) be the limit. Then \(f\) is a differentiable function, and
0800: \begin{equation}
0801: f'(x) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+1) c_{n+1} {(x-a)}^{n} ,
0802: \end{equation}
0803: where the radius of convergence of this series is \(\rho\text{.}\)
0804: 🔗
0805: 🔗
0806: Proof.
0807: Take \(0 < r < \rho\text{.}\) The series converges uniformly on \([a-r,a+r]\text{,}\) but we need uniform convergence of the derivative. Let
0808: \begin{equation}
0809: R \coloneqq \limsup_{n \to \infty} \sabs{c_n}^{1/n} .
0810: \end{equation}
0811: As the series is convergent, \(R < \infty\text{.}\) The radius of convergence \(\rho\) is \(\nicefrac{1}{R}\) (or \(\rho = \infty\) if \(R=0\)).
0812: 🔗
0813: Let \(\epsilon > 0\) be given. In Example 2.2.14, we saw \(\lim_{n\to\infty} n^{1/n} = 1\text{.}\) Hence, there exists an \(N\) such that for all \(n \geq N\text{,}\) we have \(n^{1/n} < 1+\epsilon\text{.}\) So
0814: \begin{equation}
0815: R =
0816: \limsup_{n \to \infty}
0817: \sabs{c_n}^{1/n}
0818: \leq
0819: \limsup_{n \to \infty}
0820: \sabs{n c_n}^{1/n}
0821: \leq
0822: (1+\epsilon)
0823: \limsup_{n \to \infty}
0824: \sabs{c_n}^{1/n}
0825: =
0826: (1+\epsilon)R .
0827: \end{equation}
0828: As \(\epsilon\) was arbitrary, \(\limsup_{n \to \infty} \sabs{n c_n}^{1/n} = R\text{.}\) Therefore, \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty n c_{n} {(x-a)}^{n}\) has radius of convergence \(\rho\text{.}\) Dividing by \((x-a)\text{,}\) we find \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty (n+1) c_{n+1} {(x-a)}^{n}\) has radius of convergence \(\rho\) as well.
0829: 🔗
0830: Consequently, the partial sums \(\sum_{n=0}^k (n+1) c_{n+1} {(x-a)}^{n}\text{,}\) which are derivatives of the partial sums \(\sum_{n=0}^{k+1} c_{n} {(x-a)}^{n}\text{,}\) converge uniformly on \([a-r,a+r]\text{.}\) Furthermore, the series clearly converges at \(x=a\text{.}\) We may thus apply Theorem 6.2.10, and we are done as \(r < \rho\) was arbitrary.
0831: 🔗
0832: 🔗
0833: Example 6.2.14.
0834: We could have used this result to define the exponential function. That is, the power series
0835: \begin{equation}
0836: f(x) \coloneqq \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{x^n}{n!}
0837: \end{equation}
0838: has radius of convergence \(\rho=\infty\text{.}\) Furthermore, \(f(0) = 1\text{.}\) Differentiating term by term, we find that \(f'(x) = f(x)\text{.}\)
0839: 🔗
0840: 🔗
0841: Example 6.2.15.
0842: The series
0843: \begin{equation}
0844: \sum_{n=1}^\infty n x^n
0845: \end{equation}
0846: converges to \(\frac{x}{{(1-x)}^2}\) on \((-1,1)\text{.}\)
0847: 🔗
0848: Proof: On \((-1,1)\text{,}\) \(\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n\) converges to \(\frac{1}{1-x}\text{.}\) The derivative \(\sum_{n=1}^\infty n x^{n-1}\) then converges on the same interval to \(\frac{1}{{(1-x)}^2}\text{.}\) Multiplying by \(x\) obtains the result.

0936: It is possible to define a joint limit of a double sequence \(\{ x_{n,m} \}_{n,m=1}^\infty\) of real numbers (that is a function from \(\N \times \N\) to \(\R\)). We say \(L\) is the joint limit of \(\{ x_{n,m} \}_{n,m=1}^\infty\) and write

0939: x_{n,m} = L ,
0940: \qquad
0941: \text{or}
0942: \qquad
0943: \lim_{(n,m) \to \infty}
0944: x_{n,m} = L ,
0945: \end{equation}
0946: if for every \(\epsilon > 0\text{,}\) there exists an \(M\) such that if \(n \geq M\) and \(m \geq M\text{,}\) then \(\sabs{x_{n,m} - L} < \epsilon\text{.}\)
